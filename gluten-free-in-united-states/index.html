Gluten free in the United States

A gluten-free claim on a US pack follows an FDA rule: below 20 parts per million of gluten, and no wheat, rye or barley added unless it was processed to remove gluten. Here is how to read the label, including the lines that never make the Contains statement. Save this for your next trip ✈️

Updated 18 November 2026

How the gluten-free claim works

The Food and Drug Administration writes the rule in 21 CFR 101.91. Four wordings follow it: gluten-free, no gluten, free of gluten, and without gluten.

Three things have to be true at once. The food leaves out wheat, rye and barley, and crosses such as triticale, as ingredients. An ingredient made from those grains is included only when it has been processed to remove gluten, the way some wheat starch is. Any gluten that arrives by cross-contact stays below 20 ppm, which is 20 mg of gluten per kilogram of food.

A test result under 20 ppm is one part of the rule. A food that had barley, rye or ordinary wheat flour added on purpose still misses the claim, even when the finished test looks low.

When a gluten-free food lists wheat, the label has to point from that word to a note that the wheat was processed to meet the FDA requirements for gluten-free foods.

Below 20 ppm, and no wheat, rye or barley in the recipe unless that ingredient was processed to remove gluten.

Oats are outside the FDA list of gluten-containing grains. They can sit in a gluten-free food when gluten from wheat, rye or barley cross-contact stays below 20 ppm. Whether oats suit you is a question for your clinician. The label tells you the cross-contact standard the company used.

Most packaged foods follow this FDA rule. Meat, poultry and certain egg products are regulated by the US Department of Agriculture, so a gluten-free claim on those products is handled under USDA labeling rather than 21 CFR 101.91.

Fermented and hydrolyzed foods need an extra look. Soy sauce, beer and malt vinegar are typical examples. The FDA expects the ingredients to have met the gluten-free rule before fermentation or hydrolysis, because a test of the finished product is unreliable.

Wheat is called out. Barley and rye may not be.

Wheat is a major food allergen in the United States, with milk, eggs, fish, crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, soybeans and sesame. It has to appear in the ingredient list or in a Contains statement.

Barley and rye contain gluten, and they are outside that major-allergen list. They show up in the ingredient list. A Contains line that names wheat, or names nothing about grain, can still belong to a food with barley malt or rye flour. Read the ingredients, top to bottom.

Ingredients that often carry gluten

The grain is sometimes hiding behind another name. These are the ones worth a second look.

  • Wheat under other names: spelt, farro, kamut, khorasan, durum, semolina, couscous, bulgur and graham flour.
  • Malt, malt extract, malt syrup and malt flavoring. In the US these are usually barley.
  • Barley malt vinegar. Distilled vinegar is a different ingredient, so read which vinegar is named.
  • Soy sauce and marinades made with it. Many soy sauces are brewed with wheat. Some tamari is made without wheat, and the ingredient list is what tells you.
  • Beer, ale and lager made from barley or wheat, unless the label carries a gluten-free claim.
  • Brewer's yeast, when it is a by-product of beer. Other yeasts are a different ingredient.
  • Breadcrumbs, batter, croutons, flour-thickened gravy and cream soups.
  • Seitan, which is wheat gluten, and some surimi (imitation crab) that uses wheat starch.
  • Seasoning mixes and stock bases that use wheat flour as a carrier. The ingredient list names wheat when it is there.

Eating out

A packaged gluten-free claim covers the food as sold. A kitchen is a separate question. Ask about soy sauce, flour in the gravy or soup, breadcrumbs, croutons and a shared fryer.

Questions people ask

What does gluten-free mean in the United States?

Under FDA rule 21 CFR 101.91, gluten-free, no gluten, free of gluten and without gluten all mean the same thing. The food leaves out wheat, rye and barley unless an ingredient from those grains was processed to remove gluten, and any gluten from cross-contact is below 20 ppm.

Does wheat-free mean gluten-free?

Wheat-free tells you wheat was left out. Barley, rye and malt can still be ingredients. Gluten-free is the claim that covers those grains.

Why read past the Contains line?

Wheat is a major allergen, so it has to be declared. Barley and rye contain gluten and are outside that list, so they can appear in the ingredients without showing up in the Contains statement.

Can oats be labeled gluten-free?

Yes, when any gluten from wheat, rye or barley cross-contact stays below 20 ppm. Oats themselves are not on the FDA list of gluten-containing grains. Whether you eat oats is a decision to make with your clinician.
